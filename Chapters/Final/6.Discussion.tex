\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chap:final-discussion}

This chapter interprets the findings presented in Chapter~\ref{chap:final-results} in relation to the study objectives, the existing literature, and the limits of the available evidence. It first considers the primary\acrshort{roi}-based outcome and the complementary global results, then examines the correction--preservation trade-off between the two architectures. The chapter also discusses the external technical characterization and blinded reader evaluation, before outlining the principal limitations, implications, and directions for future work. Numerical estimates and statistical results are reported in the preceding chapter and its associated tables; the emphasis here is on their scientific and clinical interpretation.

\section{Summary of the Main Findings}

 The internal evaluation supports the primary hypothesis of structural improvement in the Artifact Zone relative to the corrupted input across both architectures and all evaluated seeds (\autoref{tab:roi-delta-ssim}). The validation-selected Single Model and the Dual Model showed the same overall pattern. This localized improvement was accompanied by consistent global improvement in structural similarity, signal-to-noise ratio, and absolute error (\autoref{tab:global-ssim}, \autoref{tab:global-psnr}, \autoref{tab:global-mae}). Patient-level estimates, confidence intervals, and hypothesis tests are reported in the corresponding results tables.

 Descriptive slice-level inspection (\autoref{fig:global-improvement-histograms}) showed that unfavorable cases were uncommon. This pattern is reinforced by the\acrshort{roi} analysis (\autoref{sec:roi-evaluation}): correction was concentrated in the Artifact Zone, while changes in the Clean Zone were substantially smaller. This contrast indicates that the model did not apply indiscriminate image smoothing, but rather concentrated changes where artifacts were present.

\section{Comparison with Prior Work}

Direct numerical comparison with previously published metal artifact reduction studies is inherently limited. Reported gains depend on the dataset, artifact-severity distribution, evaluation region, and metric definition used by each study, and few works share a common benchmark. This reflects a lack of experimental standardization already noted in Chapter~\ref{chap:final-related-works}. With this caveat, the present results are broadly consistent, in direction and order of magnitude, with improvements reported by image-domain deep learning approaches for\acrshort{ctmar}. Gjesteby et al.~\cite{gjestebyDeepLearningMethods2017} and Zhang and Yu~\cite{zhangConvolutionalNeuralNetwork2018} reported that\acrshort{cnn}-based correction outperformed classical sinogram-domain methods such as NMAR. Lin et al.'s DuDoNet~\cite{linDuDoNetDualDomain2019} reported significant improvements over single-domain approaches using a more complex dual-domain design. Huang et al.~\cite{huangMultimodalFeaturefusionCT2022}, using a\acrshort{gan}-based approach evaluated on the DeepLesion dataset, also reported improvements in\acrshort{psnr} and\acrshort{ssim}. Differences in architecture, loss function, and evaluation-region definition mean that these comparisons remain indicative rather than controlled benchmarks.

The pre-specified primary hypothesis was evaluated specifically in the Artifact-ROI, directly targeting the region where the diagnostic risk of artifacts is concentrated, rather than relying exclusively on global image-quality metrics as most of the reviewed studies do. Finally, in line with the concern raised by Zech et al.~\cite{zechVariableGeneralizationPerformance2018} about performance degradation under domain shift, the selected model was additionally characterized on an independent external set of real exams from\acrshort{tcia}, a step not undertaken by any of the image-domain\acrshort{mar} studies reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chap:final-related-works}.

\section{Architectural Comparison Interpretation}

The paired comparison between architectures (\autoref{sec:paired-comparison-single-dual}) addressed the second specific objective: whether auxiliary artifact-mask supervision improves reconstruction relative to the version without mask supervision. The result was nuanced. Globally, the Single Model showed a small but statistically significant advantage across the evaluated metrics (\autoref{tab:workflow-paired-comparison}). In the Artifact Zone, this advantage was limited to structural similarity; the other reconstruction metrics did not show a significant paired difference (\autoref{tab:roi-artifact-paired-comparison}).

Conversely, in the Clean Zone, the Dual Model produced smaller absolute changes from the input across all three metrics, with a statistically significant preservation advantage after Holm correction (\autoref{tab:roi-clean-paired-preservation}). This pattern can be interpreted as a trade-off. The additional segmentation supervision appears to spatially constrain correction, making the Dual Model marginally more conservative outside the Artifact Zone. This comes at the cost of a slightly smaller correction magnitude, both globally and within the Artifact Zone itself. However, in this work the decoder was kept almost entirely shared, with the segmentation output added only at the final layer. A deeper separation between reconstruction and segmentation decoders deserves better exploration in future works.

The observed differences are small and do not appear clinically distinguishable on their own. Their statistical significance reflects a consistent paired direction across patients rather than a large effect size. Thus, auxiliary mask supervision did not produce decisively superior reconstruction; instead, it redistributed the correction--preservation trade-off in a measurable way.
\section{Anatomical Preservation and Reconstruction Safety}

Taken together, these descriptive analyses point to a consistent, although not clinically conclusive, pattern. The\acrshort{roi} partition showed that changes in the Clean Zone were a small fraction of the correction observed in the Artifact Zone (\autoref{fig:roi-boxplots}, \autoref{fig:roi-safety}). Patient-level Bland--Altman analysis of mean body intensity (\autoref{fig:bland-altman-patient-hu}) indicated a small systematic positive shift that should be monitored in future work.

As an additional characterization of the lower-performance tail, the lowest-performing test slices retained positive mean improvement across the evaluated metrics (\autoref{fig:appendix-worst-cases}), despite substantial dispersion and individual cases of degradation relative to the input. This suggests that unfavorable cases tend to be graded rather than catastrophic. They nonetheless exist and warrant qualitative inspection, provided for the extreme Artifact-Zone structural-similarity cases in \autoref{sec:residual-maps-extremes}.

A complementary exploratory analysis (\autoref{fig:appendix-artifact-load-association}) related artifact burden, measured as the patient-level artifact-area/body-area fraction, to global improvement. It suggested that the model tended to correct proportionally more in patients with greater artifact burden. However, this analysis was not pre-specified and was not corrected for multiple comparisons. It should therefore be read as a pattern worth testing further, not as independent statistical proof.

Together,\acrshort{roi} stratification, Bland--Altman calibration, characterization of the lower-performance tail, and the association with artifact burden provide a convergent but still descriptive picture. Correction is concentrated where needed, clean tissue is largely preserved with a small calibration shift, and the lower-performing slices do not show systematic anatomical distortion. Definitive assessment of clinical safety cannot rest on these internal, reference-based analyses alone. The completed external reader evaluation (\autoref{sec:external-evatluation-doctors}) provides a further, complementary line of evidence, discussed later in this chapter, though it too remains descriptive and based on a small panel.

\section{Generalization and External Evaluation}

The fourth specific objective was to investigate the generalization capacity of the selected version on real data external to training, obtained from\acrshort{tcia}. This objective can only be answered partially with the technical evidence currently available. The 15 main cases in the external set have no clean reference, so only the magnitude and variability of change between input and output could be characterized (\autoref{tab:external-input-output-global}).

These values are not directly comparable with the internal improvement deltas, which require a clean reference to quantify correction accuracy. They only indicate that the model produced changes of plausible magnitude and neither collapsed to the identity nor produced extreme distortions in the sampled external cases. Picking out, purely for illustration, the three cases with the highest and the three with the lowest input-output\acrshort{ssim} shows this variability without being a formal statistical comparison.

Thus, the available external technical evidence supports only that model behavior on real data outside the training domain is qualitatively plausible and consistent in order of magnitude with that observed internally. It does not establish artifact-removal accuracy or validated generalization without a clean reference. Whether human readers judge the external correction to be accurate and safe is addressed, within the descriptive limits of a small reader panel, by the completed blinded physician evaluation discussed in the next section.

\section{Reader Evaluation and Clinical Implications}

The blinded multi-reader evaluation (\autoref{sec:external-evatluation-doctors}) was designed with anatomical visualization as its primary outcome and artifact interference, paired preference, potential-harm flagging, complementary usefulness, and intra-reader consistency as secondary outcomes. This design suits a small panel of readers built specifically to compare input and output side by side.

Data collection was completed with six readers, one below the pre-specified target of seven and above the pre-specified minimum for useful descriptive results (\autoref{tab:external-reader-recruitment}). For the primary outcome, anatomical visualization underlying the artifact, readers favored the model output more often than the artifact input. The corresponding cluster-bootstrap interval was wide and included the no-difference value (\autoref{tab:external-primary-outcome}, \autoref{tab:external-primary-model}). The primary outcome is therefore best read as a descriptive signal favoring the output on average across this small reader--case sample, not as statistically confirmed improvement in perceived anatomical preservation.

The secondary outcomes followed a broadly consistent, although not uniform, direction. Reduced visible metal artifact was the largest and most consistent paired preference (\autoref{tab:external-secondary-artifact-intensity}). For the harm-flagging item, which probes for distortions introduced away from the artifact, the output was also preferred (\autoref{tab:external-secondary-distortion}). The meaningful minority of presentations favoring the input here should temper the otherwise favorable pattern. Clinical-interpretation preference was the least separated of the four outcomes (\autoref{tab:external-secondary-clinical-preference}). At the end of the session, reader opinions on the usefulness of a processed version were favorable for four of six readers but remain descriptive in this small sample (\autoref{tab:external-secondary-utility}).

Intra-reader exact agreement across the hidden-repeat pairs was modest for the four per-case questions (\autoref{tab:external-intra-reader-reliability}). Individual readers did not always reproduce their judgment when the same case reappeared later in the session. This limits the precision attributable to any single reader's response and reinforces that the pooled percentages describe a panel-level tendency rather than an individually reliable signal.

Taken together, the completed evaluation offers descriptive evidence from a small and purposely built reader panel judging a purposely curated case set. The model output was preferred over the artifact input more often than not for anatomical preservation, artifact-intensity reduction, and clinical-interpretation preference. This preference was strongest and most consistent for reduced artifact intensity, and least separated for clinical-interpretation preference. It does not, however, constitute a confirmatory clinical trial. The primary-outcome contrast interval included zero, the panel remained one reader below the pre-specified target, intra-reader repeat agreement was only moderate, and no correction for multiple comparisons was applied across the four per-case outcomes. Consistent with the design set out in \autoref{chap:final-methods}, these results are interpreted here strictly as an observational, paired reader comparison. They support cautious, exploratory optimism about perceived anatomical preservation and complementary usefulness. However, they do not establish, on their own, that either architecture is causally better in clinical terms, nor do they rule out that the model introduces perceptible change in tissue away from the artifact for a relevant minority of cases.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{No patient paired metadata.} The\acrshort{ctmar} Dataset provides real slices with simulated metal artifacts, but no paired patient metadata to enable patient level partitioning. The reliability of the patient-level partitioning was strengthened by structural-similarity matching of\acrshort{ctmar} slices to DeepLesion and UCLH Stroke EIT, audited using\acrshort{ms-ssim} and human visual review. This mitigated the risk of data leakage between training and evaluation. However, the matching is similarity-based retrieval rather than guaranteed pixel-level registration from the same tracked slice. Small residual anatomical differences between matched pairs cannot be fully excluded despite the audit.

\textbf{Anatomical imbalance.} The anatomical distribution was approximately 88\% \textit{body} and 12\% \textit{head} at the pair level, and 95.9\% \textit{body} and only 4.1\% \textit{head} (11 of 269 patients) at the patient level. Conclusions specific to the head region therefore rely on a small patient subset and should be interpreted cautiously.

\textbf{Patient-level sample size.} The internal test set's statistical comparisons treated the patient as the unit of analysis ($n=41$), which was enough for the main paired comparisons performed, but limited for more detailed subgroup analyses (by anatomical region, artifact severity, or implant type) beyond the exploratory association with artifact burden already identified as non-primary.

\textbf{External evaluation.} The external set is small and purposefully curated (15 main cases), has no clean reference, and is restricted to one model and seed selected after the internal test comparison of the validation-frozen candidates. The results describe change magnitude rather than correction accuracy.

\textbf{Loss-function and fine-detail preservation.} The reconstruction objective combined a pixel-wise $L_1$ term with local\acrshort{ssim}. Although this objective improved agreement with the clean internal reference, it did not explicitly optimize the preservation of noise texture, high-frequency detail, lesion conspicuity, or any task-specific diagnostic endpoint. One reader noted in an optional final comment that some processed images appeared blurred in a manner inconsistent with the density of the tissue under assessment and could be misleading if the processing-related origin of the alteration was not recognized. This single anonymous free-text observation is included as an illustrative qualitative concern, not as a quantified outcome or evidence that the loss function caused the effect.

\textbf{Risk of misleading synthetic change.} Neither the internal nor the external evaluation included lesion-level labels, microcalcification annotations, pathology reference, or a lesion-detection task. The reference-based internal analysis therefore quantified image fidelity but could not establish preservation of subtle pathological findings, while the external cases lacked a clean reference altogether. Consequently, this study cannot rule out the removal of subtle findings or the creation of anatomically plausible but misleading features by the network. Future safety validation should use lesion-annotated or pathology-linked data and a pre-specified diagnostic detection task.

\textbf{Small external reader panel.} The multi-reader evaluation compared input and output side by side and was completed with six readers and 90 case-level responses. The primary-outcome confidence interval was consequently wide and included the no-difference value. Intra-reader repeat agreement was only moderate ($50.0$--$58.3\%$), and no multiplicity correction was applied across the four secondary outcomes, as discussed in the preceding section.

\section{Implications and Future Work}

The most immediate practical implications follow directly from the identified limitations. First, now that the label-blinded physician evaluation has been completed, any further claim of perceived clinical benefit or reconstruction safety should rest on replication with a larger, ideally multi-center multi-reader, multi-case panel. Such a panel would allow the primary-outcome confidence interval to exclude the no-difference value with adequate precision. The panel-level tendencies reported here are a basis for that replication, not a substitute for it. A task-specific diagnostic study should distinguish subjective image-quality outcomes, which may use ordinal rating scales, from lesion-detection or localization outcomes, for which\acrshort{roc} or\acrshort{froc} analysis requires an independent reference standard. Second, prospective validation in larger and more diverse external patient groups would test generalization and safety more robustly than the 15 external cases currently available. Such validation should give greater representation to the head region and to clinical-interpretation assessments, where the output--input preference contrast was least separated.

From an architectural perspective, the identified trade-off between the Single Model (a slight advantage in correction magnitude) and the Dual Model (a slight preservation advantage in the Clean Zone) suggests that future work could explore ways to combine both effects. One option is adaptive weighting of reconstruction loss by the predicted artifact mask, rather than independent auxiliary supervision, to seek greater correction and greater preservation simultaneously. Testing progressively deeper separation of the reconstruction and segmentation decoders could also clarify its impact on correction and preservation.

Projection--image dual-domain MAR is a distinct future direction. The present pipeline begins with reconstructed image tensors and contains no raw projection data, sinograms, scanner geometry, or explicit physics-based data-consistency layer. A future dual-domain study would therefore require an acquisition-level dataset, a validated projection model, and a separate experimental protocol. It should not be presented as a simple modification of the current image-domain network. Such a design could combine projection-domain consistency with image-domain refinement, as in DuDoNet~\cite{linDuDoNetDualDomain2019}.

Transformer- and diffusion-based architectures could likewise be evaluated as alternative reconstruction backbones under the same patient-level split, reference-based metrics, and safety-oriented reader endpoints used here. Their ability to model longer-range context or richer image priors is a hypothesis to test, rather than evidence that they would necessarily preserve texture better than the present\acrshort{unet}. In particular, any generative model must be assessed for preservation of subtle anatomy and for misleading synthetic detail, not only for improved perceptual appearance or global image-similarity metrics.

Finally, expansion of the observer protocol (more readers, calibration or prior-training rounds, and a larger external-case panel) should accompany any future extension of the clinical evaluation, while keeping the same care taken throughout this dissertation to separate exploratory findings from confirmed ones.
